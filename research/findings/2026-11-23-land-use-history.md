## 1. The landscape around 1850
- The Mer de Glace reached its largest 19th century size in **1821**, about 40 m short of its Little Ice Age maximum around **1644**. It advanced again in **1852**, stopping about 70 m behind the 1821 moraines. (S25)
- During the Little Ice Age the glacier reached the Chamonix valley floor at about **1000 m** almost continuously. (S25) In the 18th and 19th centuries its tongue reached the hamlet of Les Bois and was called the Glacier des Bois. (S38)
- Since the 1850s it has retreated more than **2 km**. Today it is 12 km long with tributaries, about 32 km², spanning 1500 to 4000 m. (S25)
- From 1939 to 2001 its surface lowered on average **30 cm per year**. (S38)
- How we know: dated drawings and paintings (Linck, Birmann), maps (Forbes, Viollet-le-Duc), photos, written valley descriptions and moraines. (S25) **World-building note:** the 1850 world has real visual references.
- Chamonix villages were sited between cultivable land and pasture, away from avalanche and flood paths. Houses clustered tightly, which the source links to centuries of self-sufficiency. (S36)
- Before tourism the village lived mainly on farming and herding. (S28) The only crop detail found, rye and oats in the 18th century, comes from an unverified part of S36. **Marked unverified.**

## 2. How people used the high ground
Direct Chamonix or TMB pastoral history was not reachable. The closest evidence is from two other Alpine valleys (Hautes-Alpes, France and Cuneo, Italy). **Regional, not local:**
- 18th century: upland pastures mainly carried dairy chalets. (S29)
- First half of the 19th century: livestock numbers rose, driving demand for winter hay. More high pasture was rented to transhumant flocks from Provence. (S29)
- Second half of the 19th century: high meadows lost value and were abandoned, left mostly to transhumant shepherds. (S29)
- The authors argue social and economic change shaped the uplands about as much as climate did. (S29) **This is the key link to the bird story:** grazing leaving the slopes lets shrubs and trees climb, which looks like a climate signal.
- Still grazed today: the Jovet alpage in the Contamines reserve, on the TMB, has a temporary shepherd hut and a support shepherd in its fifth year. Fenced wetlands there improved once trampling and dung dropped. (S18)
- Named alpages on the TMB cols today: Charamillon (France) and Herbagères (Switzerland) at the Col de Balme. (S39, no history given)

## 3. Trade, borders and smuggling
Pass-specific history for Bonhomme, Seigne, Ferret and Balme was **not found**. What exists is from the Chablais, Haute-Savoie, north of the TMB. **Regional, not local:**
- Savoy voted to join France on 22 and 23 April 1860, about 130,839 voters, 98.5% yes. France took possession on 14 June 1860. (S34)
- A large free-trade zone covered almost all of Haute-Savoie from 1860 until a law of 16 February 1923 ended it. (S34)
- 19th and early 20th century smuggling was mostly a side job for farmers, crossing passes on foot to Switzerland, mainly for tobacco. (S34)
- Goods: ham, eggs, cheese, sausage and butter from the Swiss side; coffee, sugar, chocolate, pepper and tobacco toward France. (S34)
- Loads: under 5 kg for small carriers; organized bands of at least three, two carrying 30 to 50 kg each. (S34)
- From an unverified part of S34: cattle smuggled over passes until the early 1930s; foot smuggling in the mountains until the 1960s, mostly by night. **Marked unverified.**
- Seigne has a small barracks near the col on the Italian side (S39, photo caption, no date).

## 4. Where the trails came from
**Honest answer: not established yet.** No source reached tonight says whether TMB paths began as game trails, herders' paths, mule tracks or built routes.
- 1767: Horace-Bénédict de Saussure is said to have walked around the massif looking for a summit route. Two tour-company pages repeat this; neither cites a source, and one admits the route is unknown. **Tourism storytelling until checked against Saussure's own writing.**
- 1950 to 1952: the GR TMB was waymarked, described as the first international long-distance route. (S24) French Wikipedia gives 1951 for its creation. (S37)
- The TMB shares the GR5 between the Brevent and the Col de la Croix du Bonhomme. (S35)
- Reasonable but **unsourced** hypothesis: the cols were crossed long before tourism by herders and traders, so the TMB stitches older crossing paths together with newer connectors. The historian should test this before anything is built on it.

## 6. Refuges: from shelter to booked bed
- Gouter hut, as one example of the arc: 1854 a simple hut; 1858 a wooden hut for 4; 1906 French Alpine Club hut, 7 places; 1936 private refuge, 30 places; 1962 new building; 2014 new refuge, 120 places, over 7 million euros, solar and dry toilets. (S27) Not on the TMB, but the same story.
- Gouter today: about 9,000 overnight stays per four-month season, 55% foreign visitors. (S27)
- TMB today: 43 refuges and lodges in the keepers' association, **1,673 beds** around the massif. At least 30% of beds must stay in dormitories. (S21)
- Since 1 October 2025 agencies can book, with quotas favoring individual hikers. Summer 2026 booking opened 15 October 2025; by mid-November some places were full. (S21)
- Croix du Bonhomme refuge: build history not found.

- The Contamines reserve reports a marked and continuous rise since 2023, spreading onto side routes, off-trail, earlier in the season and into the night. (S18)

## 8. Impacts on the land
**Local evidence (qualitative):**
- Badly defined trails lead walkers to create parallel "multi-paths", i.e. braiding. UTMB volunteers list restoring them as project work, for example Le Curru, Aosta Valley. (S20)
- Droughts, heavy rain, freeze-thaw, snowmelt and floods wash paths out. (S20)
- Col du Bonhomme trail restoration (2019 opinion): approved on condition that new drainage and water diversions do not damage fragile arctic-alpine turf and its wetland plants, which had to be fenced off before work. (S19) **This is runoff in plain terms:** a trail changes where water goes.
- Lac Jovet, 20 July 2025: over 30 kg of waste removed, remains of a campfire. (S18)
- 749 infractions in the reserve in 2025, rising since 2021; mostly bivouac-hour violations. (S18)

**Measured numbers (outside the TMB, label them that way):**
- Rocky Mountains, USA: trail width grows with use, median 63.5 cm (low use) to 114.3 cm (high use). Soil lost from the trail cross-section 251.6 to 574.2 cm². Use level explained 55% of width variation. Side treads form more on steep, fall-line sections. (S30)
- Eastern USA: fall-line trails concentrate water and widen; flat trails braid and go muddy from poor drainage. Fixes: side-hill reroutes, grade reversals, drains, rock hardening. (S31)
- Triglav, Slovenia (closest alpine case): average trail width 1.4 m, from 4.5 m in forest to 0.6 m on the exposed ridge. (S32)
- Gornergrat, Switzerland, about 2800 m: trampling at Riffel Lake rose markedly over 30 years; photos from 1882 to 2015 show steady degradation of shore and fen vegetation. Regrowth is slow at altitude. (S33)

## 9. The ptarmigan on the TMB itself
**The strongest find tonight.** The Contamines reserve, crossed by the TMB at the Col du Bonhomme, has counted rock ptarmigan with pointing dogs at Jovet-les Fours since 2008 (Observatoire des Galliformes de Montagne reference zone). (S18)
- 9 August 2025: **41 adults, 0 juveniles, no broods.** In 2022, 5 broods were counted. (S18)
- The reserve reports a marked decline since monitoring began. (S18)
- Same report: grazing of grouse brood areas after chicks fledge is a priority concern. (S18)
- This puts the bird, the hikers and the herds on the same few km². It needs the avian expert's review, and the full year-by-year series should be requested.
- Not yet read: Imperio et al. 2013 (PLoS ONE), on climate plus human disturbance and local extinction of rock ptarmigan in the western Italian Alps. Directly relevant. See "Needs Caitlin to download".
