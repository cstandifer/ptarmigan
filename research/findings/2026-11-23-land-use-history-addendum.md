## 1. Where the trails came from
- **Local people knew the high ground first.** Chamois hunters, crystal hunters and mountain farmers crossed the heights before the scientists came (Balmat was a crystal hunter, Cachat a farmer). (S48)
- When tourism arrived, those locals became the guides, and their herding and hunting knowledge became the routes. The source notes that hunters followed the paths of Alpine animals. (S47) **This is the closest evidence for Caitlin's animal-path question.** It covers the high mountain routes, not specifically the TMB footpaths.
- **The TMB's western half was already a through route in the 18th century.** According to Bourrit, people went from Chamonix to Courmayeur over the **Col du Bonhomme and then the Col de la Seigne, about three days' walk**. (S48)
- **The ice may have pushed people onto that detour.** Windham, Bordier and Bourrit all record a Chamonix tradition: their grandparents could reach the Aosta valley in about **six hours** by a direct route behind Montenvers, until the growing glaciers closed it. The historian treats the old route as a hypothesis, supported by several accounts but not proven. (S48) **Story beat:** the Little Ice Age bent the human path around the mountain.
- Around the same time, Chamonix people asked priests from Annecy to exorcise the valley because the Mer de Glace was advancing. In the 18th century Mont Blanc was called "the cursed mountain". (S48)

**Transhumance paths (drailles).** These are regional, from the southern French Alps, not the TMB. (S48)
- Flocks wore their own routes, called drailles, wide enough for a whole herd.
- They follow ridges, hillsides and small valleys, and run **fairly directly up to the cols, because animals like to climb straight up the slope**.
- Low stone walls sometimes line them to keep the flock together.
- Drailles often merge with trade or military routes. Traces survive on the Col de la Vanoise path.
- Shepherds' chalets stand along the cols.

**An inference that ties history to erosion. It is mine, not a source's, and is flagged for the environmental scientist:** a path shaped by animals climbing straight up runs along the fall line. The erosion studies (S31) show that fall-line paths channel water and widen. So the oldest kind of path may also be the kind that erodes worst under heavy modern use.

## 2. Herding and trade over the cols (regional: Briançonnais and Queyras, 1740 to 1810)
- Farming and herding were the two pillars of the local economy. Herds went up to the alpages from **June to mid-October**, and some crossed the border to graze on the Piedmont side. (S48)
- Herds were mostly sheep, plus goats (despite official limits), many mules and donkeys, cattle, pigs and poultry. Queyras mules sold in large numbers to Piedmontese buyers. (S48)
- Fairs on both sides of the Alps traded wine, livestock, rye, wheat, cheese, hides, wool, textiles and copper. People crossed the cols to reach them. (S48)
- Local trade over the cols also ran on smuggling, peddling and seasonal migration of workers. (S48)
- In the 18th century the Mont Blanc high mountain was seen as a separate world from the familiar, grazed alpages. (S48)

## 3. Smuggling (regional: Dauphiné, 18th century)
- Salt was taxed differently from valley to valley, so smuggling paid. (S49) The 1715 cheap-salt privilege for the Briançonnais and Queyras was meant to revive herding. Salt was essential for livestock as well as people. (S49)
- **Savoy got its salt from Peccais, Languedoc.** (S49) This is the Savoy link.
- The Briançon salt court records **71 salt-smuggling trials between 1725 and 1760**. **Nearly 30% involved women.** More than two thirds of cases were buying salt to resell. (S49)
- Example: on 14 June 1740, three women from Les Hieres were caught near the Col du Lautaret carrying sixty pounds of salt. (S49) A pound at Briancon weighed 410 to 420 g. (S49)
- In these mountain families the men often left to work seasonally, and women ran farms and the side trade. (S49)
- Louis Mandrin (1725 to 1755) led a large tobacco-smuggling band across the Alps, buying in Switzerland and Savoy. He became a folk hero, and much of that story is legend. (S48)
- All of this is **Dauphiné, not Savoy or the TMB**. It gives the texture, not local fact.

## 4. The treeline: grazing versus warming
- Swiss Alps, 1985 to 1997, from land-use statistics: forest cover rose significantly between **1650 and 2450 m**. (S50)
- Above 1650 m, about **10%** of new forest was a true upward shift and about **90%** was filling-in below the existing line. (S50)
- Land abandonment was the dominant driver. Only **4%** of upward shifts rose above the potential regional treeline, the share the authors attribute to climate. Most shifts lay within **300 m below** the potential treeline, which points to land use. (S50)
- A citing study adds that treeline dynamics in the European Alps have been shaped by land abandonment **since 1850**. (S51)
- **Limits:** this is the abstract only, the full paper is paywalled, and it covers Switzerland, not Mont Blanc. Any claim that rests on it is marked "abstract only, regional".
- **What it means for the story:** the CREA treeline rise of about 60 m since the 1950s (S2) probably mixes both causes. The scene should not show every metre as warming.

## 5. The bird: climate and human disturbance (Imperio et al. 2013)
- Western Italian Alps, Alpe Veglia and Alpe Devero Natural Park. Breeding habitat is **2100 to 2700 m**. (S46)
- Calling cocks fell from **7.52 per km² (1997) to 2.63 per km² (2012)**, an average loss of **0.26 per km² a year**. (S46)
- The Italian population is estimated at 5,000 to 8,000 pairs, declining about 30% in a decade. The Alpine population is about 40,000 pairs. (S46)
- Snowmelt date and the start of autumn snow cover together explain 78% of the variation in growth rate. An earlier snow-free spring helps breeding. A **later autumn snow cover hurts**. (S46)
- The authors' explanation for the autumn effect: birds molt white by day length, so when snow comes late they are white on bare ground and easy prey for golden eagles. **This is a hypothesis in the paper ("possibly"), not a measured result.** It touches the CLAUDE.md open question on molt, so it goes to the avian expert. It does not settle the question.
- The disturbance studied is **ski-lift wire collisions**, not hikers. The authors also say tourist infrastructure and visitor numbers disturb birds in winter and summer, but they did not measure it. (S46)
- **Projection** (A1B scenario, PROTHEUS model): depending on the model, from steady decline to extinction, with a 50% chance by 2033 and 95% by 2040 under model M1. Adding a heavily developed ski area brings extinction within about 10 years. (S46)
- The authors recommend strict protection of habitat where the birds live now, plus careful planning or closure of hunting. (S46)
